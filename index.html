The
Argument

Editor's note
Filed under: defence

There is a specific look people give you when you say the word jazz. Somewhere between pity and being asked for directions. It is the look of somebody picturing a saxophone solo that will not end, in a room with a two-drink minimum, played by a man who owns a hat.

Fine. That version exists. It is also completely irrelevant to what has been happening for the last decade, which is that the harmony got absorbed into hip-hop production and then handed back with better drums. The J Dilla lineage did what conservatories could not: it made complicated chords feel casual. A generation grew up hearing thirteenths under rap verses and never learned to find them intimidating.

Nobody in this survey is trying to save jazz. They are just refusing to be boring about it.

So the tracks are short. Forty-four of the hundred are under three minutes, five are under ninety seconds, and one of the best things here — Reuben James's Ruby Smiles — is sixty-nine seconds long and does not feel truncated. The solo has been demoted. The loop has been promoted. What used to be a head-solos-head structure is now a texture you can put on a playlist next to a rap record without either one flinching.

The geography moved too. London gets the press — Kokoroko, Kamaal Williams, Yussef Dayes, the whole Brownswood axis — and deserves most of it. But the playlist that generated this survey keeps wandering: Richmond, Virginia, where DJ Harrison records into a house. Aarhus, where Athletic Progression name tunes after basketball moves. Budapest, Berlin, Montréal, Amsterdam, Oakland, somewhere in Italy where a band called Bo!led is making genuinely excellent records for an audience of about four hundred people.

A note on what follows. Every artist below gets a short entry, a set of credits, and one video — their best music video where one exists, and where it doesn't, the live film or the single track worth your time instead. Twenty-nine of them have a proper music video. That number is lower than it should be, and it is the most honest statistic in this whole project: this is a scene making world-class music on budgets that do not stretch to a camera.

There are no scores. Ranking these against each other would be a category error — half are two-minute sketches, half are working bands, and nobody involved entered a competition. Either the write-up makes you press play or it doesn't. Arguments to the usual address.

The Playlist

“Jamies” — 100 tracks, the source document

    Source

    Every artist in this survey was pulled from one playlist. Nothing was added, nothing was dropped for being obscure. The running order below is the running order as sequenced.

    The other
    half is a shop

    Every artist in this survey has a private shop selling things that exist once — a test pressing that never left the plant, a chart in pencil, the cymbal that cracked, two hours of somebody’s undivided attention. 140 lots. Highest bid at close takes it.

    Enter the store ↗

    What this is

    A one-issue magazine about modern jazz and jazz-adjacent hip-hop, built out of a single hundred-track playlist. Sixty artists, one video each. Written to be read top to bottom, but nobody is checking.

    How the videos were chosen

    • 1. An official music video, if one exists.
    • 2. Failing that, an official visualiser or animated video.
    • 3. Failing that, a live film — KEXP, Tiny Desk, Sofar, Church of Sound.
    • 4. Failing all of it, their best track.

    The Store

    The second half of this publication is an auction house: sixty private shops, one per artist, selling one-of-one objects and one-on-one time. It is a working prototype — the lots are invented and no money changes hands.

    Corrections

    Artist origins are given where they could be confirmed and left blank where they couldn't, rather than guessed. Everything else here is argument.

    Videos are embedded from YouTube and load only when you press play.
